# Working capital vs. term loans: which fits your business?

When a business needs funding, the right question is not simply how much money is available. It is how the money will be used, how quickly it is needed, and how repayment will fit into the business's regular cash flow. Working capital financing and term loans can both provide useful support, but they solve different problems.

## Start with the cash flow problem

Working capital is the money that keeps daily operations moving. It covers payroll, rent, supplies, inventory, and other costs that arrive before customers pay. A working capital product can help bridge a temporary gap between outgoing expenses and incoming revenue.

This category can include a business line of credit, a short-term loan, invoice factoring, or other flexible financing. The structure varies, but the common idea is access to money for operating needs rather than a single long-term project.

Imagine a contractor waiting for a customer payment while materials and employees still need to be paid. A retailer may need to replenish popular inventory before sales arrive. A service business may need room to cover a seasonal dip. In each case, the financing supports the rhythm of the business.

## How a term loan differs

A term loan provides a defined amount up front and follows a scheduled repayment plan. That structure can make sense when you have a specific investment with a clear purpose, such as purchasing equipment, renovating a location, opening a new site, or consolidating business obligations.

The predictable schedule can make planning easier because you know when payments are due and can build them into a longer-term budget. The trade-off is commitment. You receive the funds at the beginning, and repayment continues according to the agreement even if the project takes longer than expected or revenue changes.

Term financing also brings costs beyond the principal. Interest, fees, and other terms vary by product and lender, so it is important to review the complete agreement rather than focusing only on the amount offered. A payment that looks manageable in isolation should still be considered alongside payroll, rent, taxes, supplier bills, and other obligations.

## Match the structure to the purpose

Ask yourself whether the need is recurring or tied to a specific event. If cash gets tight whenever invoices are delayed or demand changes, flexible working capital may fit better. If you are making a defined investment that should benefit the business over an extended period, a term loan may be easier to plan around.

Consider how often you expect to use the funds. A line of credit can provide access when needed, but it also requires discipline. Drawing money for routine expenses without a plan to restore cash flow can make a temporary gap harder to solve. A term loan may provide clearer boundaries, but unused funds cannot usually be returned to the business as flexible capacity.

Think about the timing of revenue as well. A fixed payment can feel different during a busy month than during a quiet month. Review your actual cash flow pattern and leave room for ordinary surprises before choosing a repayment structure.

## Prepare before you compare

Gather recent business bank statements, a simple view of revenue and expenses, and a clear explanation of how the funds will be used. Be ready to discuss time in business, existing obligations, and the payment your cash flow can reasonably support. Accurate information helps a lender evaluate the request and helps you compare products on their full terms.
